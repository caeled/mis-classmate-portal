Launchpad edition · Added 3 November 2026 · The starting page for new MIS students; everything else in the portal goes deeper.

MIS = People + Technology + Business

Management Information Systems is how companies use data, software, and systems to make decisions. Not the technology alone, not the business alone: the place where the two meet, and where most real-world technology successes and failures are actually decided.

7-Day Quick Start

  • Day 1–2: What is MIS? Work through Section 1 below, skim the semester roadmap, and look over the career map.
  • Day 3–4: SQL + Excel basics. Start the Skills Lab progression.
  • Day 5: Read 2 MIS Quarterly abstracts using the 15-minute reading method.
  • Day 6: Pick 1 research question from the 12 starter topics.
  • Day 7: Build 1 mini-project (a budget dashboard is the classic first one) and save it in your workspace.

1. What is MIS?

Employers hire MIS graduates because they can talk to both managers and developers: requirements, SQL, dashboards, projects, ERP/CRM, cloud, AI. A computer science graduate builds the system; an MIS graduate makes sure it is the right system, that people actually use it, and that it changes a decision for the better.

The core pipeline of the field:

Business Problem → Data → Analysis → Dashboard → Decision

If any arrow in that chain is broken (the wrong problem, dirty data, an analysis nobody trusts, a dashboard nobody opens) the decision at the end is broken too. MIS is the discipline of keeping the whole chain working.

FieldFocusCore question
MISSystems + business processHow should this organization use technology to work and decide better?
Computer ScienceAlgorithms + software engineeringHow do we build correct, efficient software?
AnalyticsStatistics + forecastingWhat do the data say, and what is likely to happen next?

Start-of-program checklist

  • ☐ Explain MIS in 2 sentences (try it out loud; use the definition at the top of this page)
  • ☐ Know your degree requirements (core courses, electives, any capstone or thesis track)
  • ☐ Email your advisor: introduce yourself and ask what they wish new students did in semester one
  • ☐ Set up library access and a Google Scholar profile/alerts
  • ☐ Pick 1 research area from Section 3
  • ☐ Start SQL and Excel (see Section 5)
  • ☐ Join 1 club (an IS/IT student chapter, data club, or security club all count)

2. Roadmap

  • Semester 1: Intro MIS, Statistics, Excel, Python → Project: personal budget dashboard
  • Semester 2: SQL & Databases, Systems Analysis → Project: club database + ER diagram
  • Semester 3: Networking/Cloud, Web Development, Power BI/Tableau → Project: sales dashboard
  • Semester 4+: Cybersecurity, ERP/SAP, AI, Project Management, Internship + Capstone (or thesis)

Pick electives by interest

  • Data track: data warehousing, business intelligence, machine learning for business, data governance. Feeds the BI/Data Analyst roles in Section 6.
  • Security track: cybersecurity fundamentals, risk and compliance, security governance. See the portal's Security & Governance page.
  • Building track: web and application development, cloud architecture, ERP configuration. See the Microsoft and Google ecosystem pages.
  • Leading track: project management, enterprise architecture, IT strategy. See Enterprise Architecture.

3. Research Topics (12)

Twelve starter topics, each with a working research question and a method that fits it. Swap in your own organization, campus, or region to make any of them yours.

Data & AI

  • Chatbots for academic advising. Question: Do students who use an advising chatbot register earlier and with fewer errors than students who do not? Method: compare registration timing and error rates for chatbot users vs. non-users over two terms; survey both groups on trust.
  • Retail dashboards. Question: Which dashboard design helps store managers spot a sales problem fastest? Method: build two dashboard variants over the same retail dataset; time managers finding seeded problems in each; interview them on what they looked at first.
  • Student dropout prediction. Question: Which early signals (attendance, LMS logins, first-assignment grades) best predict a student leaving before week 8? Method: logistic regression on a de-identified institutional dataset; report accuracy and false-positive costs, not just the headline score.
  • Hiring bias audit. Question: Does an AI resume-screening tool pass or fail candidates differently across demographic groups? Method: submit matched synthetic resumes that differ in one attribute at a time; compare pass rates; document the audit trail.

Systems

  • ERP failures (Nike, Hershey). Question: What did the famous ERP failures have in common that the successful rollouts did not? Method: structured case comparison of the public post-mortems: scope, timeline pressure, data migration, training, go-live strategy.
  • Blockchain food traceability. Question: Does blockchain traceability actually shorten food-recall times, or is the gain from the process cleanup around it? Method: compare published recall case studies with and without blockchain; interview one supply-chain practitioner if accessible.
  • SME cloud migration. Question: What makes a small business's cloud migration succeed in year one? Method: survey or interview 8–12 small businesses that migrated; code the answers for cost surprises, downtime, and staff training.

People

  • Two-factor authentication usability. Question: Which 2FA method do non-technical users complete fastest with the fewest lockouts? Method: timed usability test (think-aloud) with app codes, SMS codes, and hardware keys on common tasks.
  • E-commerce impulse buying. Question: Which interface patterns (countdowns, low-stock warnings, one-click reorder) most increase unplanned purchases? Method: survey plus a screenshot-based experiment where participants shop a mock store under different patterns.
  • Slack/Teams overload. Question: How does message volume relate to perceived overload and after-hours work? Method: one-week diary study or survey of a class or team; correlate channel count and notification settings with reported stress.
  • Banking app UX. Question: Where do users fail or hesitate in a mobile banking app's five most common tasks? Method: moderated usability sessions; count task completions, errors, and hesitation points per screen.
  • Fitness app retention. Question: Which features predict whether a fitness app user is still active at day 30? Method: analyze a public fitness-app dataset or run a small cohort survey; compare streaks, social features, and reminder settings.

The paper template

[Technology] + [Outcome] + [Context] gives you the title and the spine of the paper:

  • Turn it into one clear research question.
  • Find 3 papers that bear on it (start in Section 4).
  • Choose a method that fits the question: interviews, a survey, or an existing dataset.
  • Present findings with at least one chart a manager could read in 30 seconds.
  • State your limitations plainly: sample, setting, what your method cannot show.

For the full research workflow (search logging, evidence matrices, citation tracing) use the Research Workbench, and for model-by-model help use the Prompt Library.

4. Library

The journals that define the field

  • MIS Quarterly: the flagship MIS journal; start with abstracts and executive summaries.
  • Information Systems Research (ISR): rigorous IS research from INFORMS.
  • Journal of Management Information Systems (JMIS): management-focused IS research.
  • Journal of the Association for Information Systems (JAIS): the AIS flagship journal.
  • Harvard Business Review and MIT Sloan Management Review: where research meets practice; excellent for framing and real cases.

Free and library access

  • Free: Google Scholar, AIS eLibrary, SSRN, arXiv, Kaggle (datasets), data.gov (open government data).
  • Through your university library: EBSCO, ProQuest, IEEE Xplore, and the ACM Digital Library. More sources are catalogued on Wiki & Libraries.

The 15-minute reading method

  • Minutes 0–4: abstract + conclusion. Decide if the paper answers a question you care about.
  • Minutes 4–8: figures and tables. In a good paper, the figures are the findings.
  • Minutes 8–13: the research question, the method, and the headline finding, in the authors' own terms.
  • Minutes 13–15: write a 3-sentence summary in your own words and file it in your workspace. If you cannot summarize it, you have not read it yet.

5. Skills Lab

SQL (in order)

  • SELECT, WHERE, ORDER BY: answering one question from one table.
  • JOINs: answering questions that live in more than one table.
  • Subqueries, GROUP BY, and window functions: comparisons and running totals.
  • Project: the Chinook database (a digital music store). Answer ten business questions about it in SQL and write up each one in a sentence a manager would understand.

Excel → BI

  • Excel: PivotTables, XLOOKUP, and Power Query until cleaning a messy CSV feels routine.
  • Then Power BI or Tableau: same data, real dashboards.
  • Project: one KPI dashboard (sales, budget, or your own tracking data) that updates when the data changes.

Python

  • pandas and matplotlib: load, clean, chart.
  • Automate one boring Excel task you actually have.
  • Project: a small forecast notebook (even a naive one) with an honest paragraph on where it is wrong.

Certifications that pair with the degree

  • Project: CAPM or Scrum (PSM I).
  • Security: CompTIA Security+.
  • Cloud: Microsoft AZ-900 or AWS Certified Cloud Practitioner.
  • Data: Microsoft PL-300 (Power BI Data Analyst) or a Tableau credential.

7. Workspace

Keep one workspace (a notebook, a folder, or the Research Workbench evidence matrix) with three sections. Everything in Sections 1–6 lands here eventually.

Saved topics

  • One line per topic: title, the question, and why it interests you.
  • Star the one you picked on Day 6. Park the rest without guilt; parked topics are future papers.

Tasks

  • This week's three tasks only. When one finishes, promote the next from your course syllabi.
  • Every task gets a visible next action: "read MISQ abstract," not "do research."

Notes

  • One note per paper: citation, the 3-sentence summary from the 15-minute method, one quote with a page number.
  • One note per project: what you built, what broke, what you would do differently. That reflection is what experience-based programs grade and what employers actually ask about.
