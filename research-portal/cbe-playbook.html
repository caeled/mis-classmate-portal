Playbook edition · Added 3 November 2026 · Written from a working student's first weeks in a competency-based MIS program. Always confirm details against your own program handbook; policies differ by school and change.

How this format is different

In a traditional course, you serve time (weeks, lectures, deadlines set by someone else) and then get tested on what the time was supposed to teach you. In a competency-based program, the assessment is the course. You advance by demonstrating a defined competency (a skill, a body of knowledge, a professional behavior) well enough to satisfy its rubric. Seat time is not the product. Demonstrated ability is.

That single difference flips almost every habit school taught you. The students who struggle in this format are usually not the ones who know the least. They are the ones still studying as if there were a final exam at the end of a semester, while the program is quietly waiting for them to just demonstrate what they already can do.

Rule 1: The rubric is the syllabus

Before you read a single chapter, open the assessment and read its rubric line by line. The rubric tells you exactly what will be judged, at what level, in what form. Every hour of study that is not aimed at a rubric line is a guess.

  • Copy each rubric criterion into your notes as a heading. Your deliverable will be built under those headings.
  • Note the performance level words ("describes," "analyzes," "evaluates," "designs"). They are verbs with a workload attached. Analyzing costs more than describing; budget for it.
  • If a criterion confuses you, that is a question for your instructor or mentor now, not after your first submission.

Rule 2: Attempt early; treat "not yet" as reconnaissance

In this format, an early attempt is the cheapest diagnostic there is. A result of "not yet competent" comes back with assessor feedback that tells you precisely which rubric lines missed and why. That feedback is worth more than a week of unfocused reading, because it converts a vague subject ("databases") into a specific repair list ("your normalization example stops at 2NF; the rubric wants 3NF with a worked example").

  • Attempt when you believe you can satisfy most of the rubric, not when you feel perfectly ready. Perfectly ready is a feeling this format never quite gives you.
  • Revision is the design, not a penalty. Professionals revise deliverables against client criteria their whole careers; the program is simulating that, not punishing you.
  • Keep every round of feedback. Patterns in feedback across courses are a map of your own habits, and they are gold for later reflections and portfolios.

Rule 3: Your experience is evidence

Experience-based and competency-based programs exist because adults arrive knowing things. The mistake experienced students make is discounting their own history ("that was just my job") and writing generic, textbook-flavored submissions, when the strongest submission they own is a real one: a system they actually kept running, a failure they actually recovered, a decision they actually made.

  • For each competency, ask: "Where have I already done this, or watched it done up close?" Build the answer around that first; use coursework to fill the gaps and supply the formal vocabulary.
  • Concrete beats general. Names of methods, tools, constraints, and outcomes (sanitized where confidentiality requires) read as competence. Abstractions read as summary.
  • Ask your program about prior-learning assessment (PLA) and transfer options early. Documented experience can sometimes satisfy requirements outright, and the time to learn that is week one, not year two.
  • Keep receipts as you go: project summaries, metrics, commendations, artifacts. Future-you, assembling a portfolio or a scholarship application, will thank present-you.

Rule 4: Work backwards from the deliverable

  • Outline first: rubric criteria become your section headings before you write a paragraph.
  • Research to the outline: every source you gather should attach to a heading. The Research Workbench and the 15-minute reading method keep this fast.
  • Draft in your own voice: your experience (Rule 3) is the spine; sources are the ribs.
  • Cite as you write with the APA 7 cheat sheet, not in a panic at the end.
  • Final pass against the rubric: read your submission and the rubric side by side, criterion by criterion, and fix what you find before an assessor finds it.

Rule 5: Pace is a strategy, not a mood

Most competency-based programs run on subscription or term pricing, which means steady completions are literally worth money: every competency finished inside a term you have already paid for lowers your cost per credit. Bursts and stalls are the expensive pattern.

  • One competency at a time, finished, beats three competencies at 80%.
  • Set a weekly floor you can hold on your worst week (for example, two focused sessions), not a heroic pace you can hold for nine days.
  • When life interrupts, shrink the session, not the streak. Twenty minutes of rubric work keeps the thread loaded in your head.
  • Track completions somewhere visible. In this format, visible progress is the motivation system; there is no class schedule manufacturing it for you.

Common traps

  • Reading everything before writing anything. The material is a reference library, not a queue. Rule 1 exists to break this.
  • Studying for the exam in your head. There is no hidden test beyond the assessment in front of you. Satisfy the rubric; the rubric is public.
  • Hiding your experience. Writing like a generic student when you have real work to draw on wastes your one unfair advantage.
  • Treating feedback as a verdict. Assessor comments are a repair list written by the person who will re-read the revision. Use them literally.
  • Going silent. Mentors and instructors in these programs expect contact. A two-line question early beats a two-week stall.

The one-page workflow

  • ☐ Open the assessment. Read the rubric. Copy criteria into your notes as headings.
  • ☐ Map your experience: where have you done this before? List artifacts and examples.
  • ☐ Identify the gaps between your experience and the rubric's verbs. Research only the gaps.
  • ☐ Draft under the rubric headings, citing as you go (APA cheat sheet open).
  • ☐ Side-by-side check: submission vs. rubric, line by line.
  • ☐ Submit. Log the date and what you sent in your workspace.
  • ☐ On feedback: convert every comment into a checklist item, revise, resubmit.
  • ☐ On completion: file the artifact and one paragraph of reflection. That paragraph is future portfolio and scholarship material.
